Summit vs TrainingPeaks: one shows you the data, the other decides

TrainingPeaks gave endurance sport the vocabulary it thinks in. The question for a self-coached cyclist is who moves Thursday when Wednesday goes wrong.

By Jordi Espanyol · 5 Aug 2026 · 8 min read · TrainingPeaks facts checked 5 Aug 2026

Start with what TrainingPeaks is, because understating it would be silly.

TrainingPeaks did something almost no product manages: it gave a whole sport its shared language. When a coach tells you your fitness is 82 and your form is +6, they are speaking TrainingPeaks. Chronic and acute load and the balance between them — CTL, ATL and TSB, drawn together on the Performance Management Chart — are how endurance coaching has thought about training for two decades, and TrainingPeaks' own help centre is still where most riders go to find out what the letters mean (Fitness (CTL)). A vocabulary adopted that widely stops being a feature and becomes infrastructure. Nothing below takes that away.

It is also more than a chart. TrainingPeaks tracks health metrics alongside the training ones, HRV among them (trainingpeaks.com/athlete-features/, checked 5 August 2026), so the honest version of this comparison is not "they measure, we measure better". Both of us end up holding a number about your body. The argument is about what happens to your week once that number exists.

Because at its core TrainingPeaks is a dashboard plus a workspace for a human coach. It shows you your training in more depth than almost anything else in the sport, and then it waits for a person to decide. Usually that person is your coach. If you are self-coached, that person is you — at 22:40, tired, deciding whether tomorrow's threshold session is still a good idea.

Summit decides.

In short

TrainingPeaks shows; Summit decides. TrainingPeaks gives you — and the coach you work with — the deepest view of your training in the sport, and leaves the call to a human. Summit generates the plan and then rewrites it around your recovery and the hours you actually have this week, deterministically, with no chatbot and no AI anywhere in it. If you have a coach, TrainingPeaks stays the hub and Summit sits beside it.

A dashboard shows you; a plan has to decide

This is the whole argument, so it goes first.

TrainingPeaks answers "what has happened to me" better than anything else a cyclist can open. Fitness rising, fatigue spiking, form dropping into a hole three weeks before the event — it is all there, drawn clearly, with the numbers to back it. What it does not do, by design, is act on it. The chart is an instrument panel. Someone still has to fly the plane.

If you have a coach, that is exactly right, and it is why the product is shaped that way: the platform's job is to give a professional the clearest possible picture and get out of the way. But most amateur cyclists do not have a coach. They have a spreadsheet, a plan they downloaded in January, and a Tuesday that got eaten by work. And a beautiful chart does not move Thursday's intervals. It just shows you, later, that Thursday went badly.

Summit is holding the plan, which is what lets the numbers act. A strong morning and today's session stands, or grows. An average one and it is left exactly as planned — quietly downgrading sessions every time a number dips is how riders lose fitness while feeling responsible. A genuinely bad morning and the hard work comes out but the hours on the bike stay, because on a rough day it is the intensity that costs you. Tell it you only have ninety minutes on Saturday and the week rebuilds around ninety minutes. Report an illness or an injury and the day stops entirely, with the key session rescheduled rather than quietly disappearing.

None of that is AI and none of it is a chatbot. The engine is deterministic: every call traces back to a codified sports-science rule applied to your own data, so you can see why it decided what it decided. That is also the part Summit charges for — the plan that rearranges itself is Premium. The reading of your body is not.

The vocabulary is the same, and Summit speaks it

Nothing about "the engine decides" means the numbers get hidden. Summit is built on the same load model the sport already agreed on: chronic and acute training load and the form balance between them (CTL, ATL, TSB), plus eFTP, critical power, your power curve, and per-activity analysis of what a session actually was rather than how hard it felt. That is what tells you whether you are peaking on the right weekend or arriving flat, and it is Premium.

It also has to leave the app. Summit reads rides from Strava and Hammerhead Karoo, and exports workouts to Karoo and — via intervals.icu — to Garmin, Zwift and MyWhoosh, so the session the engine wrote is on the head unit on Saturday morning. Export is Premium too. Race-day fuelling is in the app; day-to-day nutrition that follows your training is on the roadmap, not shipped.

What Summit does not have is a TrainingPeaks connection. There is no sync between the two, in either direction. If your training life is already centred on TrainingPeaks, that is a real friction and it is better said plainly here than discovered later.

What moves the plan is your recovery — and that part is free

Summit reads heart-rate variability, resting heart rate and sleep from Apple Health. Whatever wrote them there is the sensor: an Apple Watch, a Garmin, an Oura ring, or anything else that writes to Health. Worth being precise about one thing, because it gets assumed: there is no Summit watch app. Nothing to install on the watch, no complication, no on-wrist screen. The watch measures; the iPhone reads. And an Apple Watch is not required — any wearable that writes those metrics works.

Four things cost nothing in Summit and are not scheduled to start costing anything:

  • the daily recovery score;
  • the sleep score with its full breakdown;
  • the recovery trend over time;
  • the Home and Lock Screen widgets.

That is a free tier, not a free trial. There is no counter running out and no card to enter. Premium — training load, power analysis, adaptive plans, workout export, race-day nutrition — is a separate thing, and Premium is what has the 14-day trial. Keeping those two apart matters: a "free" that quietly expires is a different product from one that does not.

What each one costs

TrainingPeaks, as listed on 5 August 2026: Premium about $134.99/year billed annually, or about $19.95/month billed monthly, and a free Basic plan that TrainingPeaks describes as including workout tracking, device syncing and limited planning. Coach Edition starts at $21.99/month. Prices change and vary — trainingpeaks.com/pricing/ and trainingpeaks.com/pricing/for-coaches/ are the authority, not this page.

Summit publishes no prices of its own. Apple sets Premium per storefront, so the App Store listing is the only source that is right in your country. What is free is on the plans page.

If you have a coach, TrainingPeaks is still the hub

Summit has no coach side. No roster, no athlete inbox, no way for someone else to write into your calendar and watch what you did with it. That is not an oversight being fixed next quarter; it is a different product. A coach knows things no engine has — what happened at work this month, how you race, what you are afraid of on a descent — and a rider who has one should keep the platform that coach works in.

Which is why the useful framing is not "instead of". It is: Summit is built for the rider who was never going to hire a coach, and who has been self-coaching out of a chart and a downloaded plan. If that describes you, the thing you are missing is not more data. It is someone to move Thursday.

Where TrainingPeaks wins

Not a token section. These are the reasons to stay on TrainingPeaks, and several of them are decisive.

The coach workspace

This is the part Summit does not attempt at all. Coach accounts, athlete rosters, plans written into a shared calendar, comments on individual workouts, Coach Match for finding a coach in the first place (trainingpeaks.com/pricing/for-coaches/, checked 5 August 2026). Two decades of coaches have shaped that workspace around how coaching actually works. If a person is going to write your training, this is where they will want to do it.

The plan marketplace

TrainingPeaks runs a marketplace where coaches publish training plans and, in TrainingPeaks' own words to coaches, "sell it to our audience of athletes" (same page, checked 5 August 2026). That means you can buy a structured, named, human-authored plan for your exact event from a coach you chose, and follow it. Summit generates a plan; it does not offer you a catalogue of them to pick from.

Integrations with years of proving behind them

Devices, trainers and third-party tools have been syncing into TrainingPeaks for a very long time, and that maturity is worth something specific: the edge cases are already found. In July 2026 Garmin announced it had acquired TrainingPeaks and TrainHeroic (Garmin newsroom, 22 July 2026), which if anything points at that side getting tighter rather than looser.

The PMC is what coaches actually think in

Summit computes the same load model, but the Performance Management Chart as TrainingPeaks draws it is the reference implementation — the picture everyone in the conversation has already seen. If you talk about training with a coach, a club or a forum, you will be talking about that chart. Speaking the standard dialect fluently is a real advantage.

Multi-sport

TrainingPeaks covers cycling, running, triathlon, rowing and more (trainingpeaks.com/athlete-features/, checked 5 August 2026), and one calendar holding a swim, a run and a ride is exactly what a triathlete needs. Summit is cycling only, deliberately and for the foreseeable future. If your week is not mostly a bike, this comparison is already over.

It is not iPhone-only

TrainingPeaks runs in a browser and has its own athlete app (trainingpeaks.com/app-for-athletes/). Summit is iPhone only, iOS 26 or later, with no Android version and no web version to announce. If you do not carry an iPhone, the comparison ends here and the answer is TrainingPeaks.

Stop reading the chart. Let it move the week.

Install Summit, allow it to read your Health data, and connect Strava. Within a few mornings you have a recovery score, a full read of your sleep, and a plan that changes when they do. Free, permanently, with no trial to expire — deterministic sports-science maths running on your phone.

Questions people actually ask

Is Summit a TrainingPeaks alternative?

Only for one of the two jobs TrainingPeaks does. If you are self-coached and what you want is a cycling plan that decides for itself and rearranges when your week or your recovery changes, Summit does that and it is the thing TrainingPeaks deliberately leaves to a person. If what you want is the workspace you share with a coach, the plan marketplace, or a multi-sport calendar, Summit does not replace any of it.

Can I use Summit and TrainingPeaks together?

Nothing stops you, but be clear about what does and does not connect: there is no direct Summit to TrainingPeaks integration. Summit reads rides from Strava and Hammerhead Karoo, and exports workouts to Karoo and, via intervals.icu, to Garmin, Zwift and MyWhoosh. If your coach lives in TrainingPeaks, that stays where the two of you work; Summit is what reads your recovery and decides your day.

Does Summit replace a coach?

No, and it is not sold as one. A coach knows things no engine has: what happened at work this month, how you race, what you are afraid of. Summit is built for riders who are not going to hire one, and it works alongside a coach rather than instead of one. It is also not an AI and not a chatbot: the engine is deterministic, and every call traces back to a codified sports-science rule applied to your own data.

How much does TrainingPeaks cost in 2026?

As listed on 5 August 2026, TrainingPeaks Premium for athletes is about $134.99 per year billed annually, or about $19.95 per month billed monthly, and there is a free Basic plan that TrainingPeaks describes as including workout tracking, device syncing and limited planning. Coach Edition starts at $21.99 per month. Prices change and vary, so trainingpeaks.com/pricing is the authority. Summit publishes no prices of its own: Apple sets Premium pricing per storefront, so the App Store listing is the only honest source.

Is Summit's recovery score really free, or is it a trial?

Free, permanently. The daily recovery score, the sleep score with its full breakdown, the recovery trend and the Home and Lock Screen widgets have no gate on them and no counter running out. Premium is a separate thing, and Premium is what has a 14-day trial.
