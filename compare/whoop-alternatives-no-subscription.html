Whoop alternatives with no subscription: six that work

Whoop's membership includes the strap, so dropping the subscription means changing devices — or using the one already on your wrist. Here is what that actually looks like, ranked by who each one suits.

By Jordi Espanyol · 6 Aug 2026 · 8 min read · Third-party facts checked 6 Aug 2026

If you want a daily recovery score with no recurring payment, buy a device once — a Garmin, an Apple Watch, a Polar band — and read it in that company's own free app.

That is the answer. The rest of this page is which one, and why "a Whoop alternative with no subscription" is a slightly different question from the one most people think they are asking.

Whoop's membership is not a software fee bolted onto hardware you already bought. It is how the hardware is paid for: every tier includes the strap. As listed on 5 August 2026, WHOOP One is $199/year, WHOOP Peak $239/year and WHOOP Life $359/year, device included — whoop.com/us/en/membership/ is the authority, not this page, and prices vary by country. So dropping the subscription cannot mean "the same strap without the fee". It means owning a measuring device outright instead, or using one already on your wrist.

Reframed like that, the shortlist gets short, honest, and mostly made of hardware.

Quick answer

Buying something new? A Garmin. Its daily energy read, Body Battery, lives in the free Garmin Connect app, and when Garmin launched its paid tier it stated plainly that all existing Garmin Connect features and data stay free. One purchase, no recurring fee for the recovery read.

Already own an Apple Watch? You have most of this already. Apple's own Vitals app gives you an overnight read and tells you when several metrics fall outside your normal range — nothing to install, nothing to pay.

Ride a bike? Then the app on top matters more than the strap, and entry 5 is the one written for you: Summit's recovery and sleep score is free, permanently, on the wearable you already own. It is cycling-only and iPhone-only, so if you run, swim, lift or carry an Android phone, your answer is one of entries 1 to 3 instead.

What "no subscription" actually costs

Neither model is free. What changes is when you pay and what you keep.

A membership is a rental: hardware plus software plus replacement, on one recurring line. Buying a watch is a purchase: hardware once, and — in every case below — a companion app that costs nothing today. The catch on the buy-once side is that a device ages on your wrist while the software around it keeps moving, and none of these companies has promised that every future feature will be free. Garmin's statement, the strongest of the lot, is carefully about existing features.

So read it as a purchase, not a guarantee. And before you spend anything, open the vendor's own page and check today's terms — every figure below carries the date we checked it, because that is the only responsible way to publish a number about someone else's product.

1. A Garmin watch — Body Battery, in Garmin Connect

What it is: a GPS watch or band you buy outright, paired with Garmin's own companion app (Garmin Connect™, published by Garmin International, free to download — checked 6 August 2026).

Garmin's equivalent of a recovery score is Body Battery, an energy figure that climbs with rest and falls with stress and training, and it sits in the free app alongside everything else the watch records. Garmin does now sell a premium tier, Garmin Connect+, and the announcement of it is the reason this entry is first: the company stated, in its own newsroom, that "all existing features and data in Garmin Connect will remain free" (27 March 2025). Read it precisely — it is a statement about what already exists, not a promise about everything Garmin will ever ship — but it is still the clearest commitment on this page. Garmin Connect is also published for Android, which is more than most of what follows.

Best for: most people leaving Whoop. One purchase, no recurring fee for the daily read, and the same device records your training instead of only watching you recover from it.

2. An Apple Watch — Apple's own Vitals app

What it is: a watch you already own, most likely, plus software Apple ships with it. Nothing to install.

Apple is unusually specific about what Vitals does. Wear the watch to bed and it estimates five overnight metrics — heart rate, respiratory rate, blood oxygen, wrist temperature and sleep duration — establishes your typical range for each, and if several land outside it you get a notification the next morning with possible causes. It needs current iOS and watchOS, Sleep Focus and sleep tracking switched on, and seven nights to learn your baseline (Apple, "Track your overnight vitals with Apple Watch", published 15 September 2025). Note what is on that list and what is not: heart-rate variability is not one of the five, although Apple Watch does calculate HRV and it appears in Health. Vitals is an outlier detector, not a training decision — it tells you something is off, not what to do about it.

Best for: iPhone owners who already have the watch and want an overnight read with nothing installed and nothing paid.

3. A Polar band — Nightly Recharge, in Polar Flow

What it is: a Polar wearable plus Polar Flow (Polar Electro Oy, free to download — checked 6 August 2026).

Polar's recovery figure is Nightly Recharge, which the company describes as combining your sleep score with heart rate, HRV and breathing data. What makes Polar worth naming on a page about subscriptions is how directly it addresses the question: its Polar Loop page says "No monthly fees. No locked features. Full access to every feature from day one," and of the app, "100% Free. No hidden fees." The Loop is also the closest thing here to Whoop's form factor — a screen-free band you sleep in rather than a watch.

Best for: someone who wants the Whoop shape, a band with nothing to look at, on a single payment.

4. An Oura Ring — the honest edge case

What it is: a ring you buy, plus the Oura app (Oura Health Oy, free to download — checked 6 August 2026) and a membership.

Oura has a subscription, so it does not belong on this page — except that what survives without one is more than most people assume, and leaving it out would be the dishonest choice. Oura's own Member Care page states the membership is "$5.99/month and $69.99/year in the US", and that without an active membership "you'll only be able to see your three daily Oura scores (Readiness, Activity, and Sleep), ring battery, basic profile information, app settings, and the Explore content." The daily readiness number itself does not disappear; everything that explains it does. If your actual requirement is one number every morning having paid once for the ring, that is a supported configuration — but go and read those terms yourself before you buy, because they belong to Oura and Oura can change them.

Best for: someone who wants a ring rather than a wrist device and genuinely only needs the daily scores.

5. Project Summit — free, on the wearable you already own

What it is: not a device. An iPhone app that reads heart-rate variability, resting heart rate and sleep out of Apple Health — whatever wrote them there, Apple Watch, Garmin, Oura or something else — and turns them into a daily recovery score and a full sleep breakdown.

The reason it is on a hardware page: if you already wear something, the cheapest good answer to "how recovered am I" may be a free app rather than a new strap. In Summit the daily recovery score, its 30-day trend, the sleep score with its full breakdown and the Home and Lock Screen widgets are free and stay free. That is a free tier, not a trial — no counter, no card. Premium is a separate thing (training load, power analysis, the prescribed session, workout export, race-day fuelling), and its 14-day trial is an introductory offer for new subscribers, not something the free tier expires into.

Now the limits, before you install anything. Summit is built for cyclists, it is iPhone only and it needs iOS 26 or later. If you run, swim, lift or carry an Android phone, entries 1 to 3 are your answer and this one is not. There is also no Summit watch app — no complication, nothing on your wrist. The watch is the sensor, the iPhone does the reading, and an Apple Watch is not required: any wearable that writes those metrics to Apple Health works, though only the Apple Watch writes the complete set Summit uses — heart-rate variability, resting heart rate, sleep, respiratory rate, blood oxygen and overnight wrist temperature — so anything else gives you a score from a partial picture.

Best for: cyclists who already wear something and want the number to change what they ride, not just what they know.

6. HRV4Training — buy the app once, wear nothing

What it is: HRV4Training (A.S.M.A. B.V.), listed at $9.99 on the US App Store on 6 August 2026 — a purchase, not a membership.

Its own listing describes it as "the first scientifically validated Heart Rate Variability (HRV) app" and says it "does not require a heart rate monitor, since it can accurately assess your HRV using the phone's camera", while also working with Apple Watch, the Oura ring and compatible Bluetooth sensors. One caveat we will not paper over: an App Store price covers the download, and Apple's public data does not say whether anything optional sits on top of it. The listing itself is the authority — read it before you buy.

Best for: someone who wants a morning HRV measurement and no new hardware at all.

Where Whoop still wins

Three of them, and they are the reasons people pay.

One device, one dataset. Whoop owns the sensor, the sampling and the model. Nothing to permit, nothing to sync, no gap that turns out to be a Health setting you switched off six months ago. Every buy-once option above is only as good as what its hardware actually records and shares.

The hardware is included, and stays included. No deciding which wearable to buy, no working out whether it writes the right metrics anywhere. One line, device supplied.

It is easier to sleep in than a watch. A fabric band with no screen is less watch than a watch, and a recovery score you actually collect every night beats a better one you collect four nights a week.

If none of that outweighs the recurring line for you, entry 1 is where to start — and if you ride, read what "free" really means among iPhone recovery apps before you install anything on top.

If the bike is the reason you were asking

Summit reads the wearable you already own through Apple Health and gives you a daily recovery score and a full sleep breakdown — free, permanently, and not a trial. No device to buy, no membership, deterministic sports-science maths running on your phone. Cyclists, iPhone, iOS 26 or later.

Questions people actually ask

Is there a Whoop alternative with no subscription at all?

Yes, if you count buying the device. Garmin, Apple and Polar all put their daily recovery read in a companion app that is free to download, so once you own the hardware there is no recurring fee for that number. What does not exist is the Whoop strap without the Whoop membership: every tier includes the device, which is how the hardware is paid for.

Is Body Battery free in Garmin Connect?

Garmin Connect is free to download (publisher Garmin International, checked 6 August 2026), and when Garmin announced its paid Garmin Connect+ tier on 27 March 2025 it stated that "all existing features and data in Garmin Connect will remain free". That is a statement about existing features rather than a promise about future ones, so check Garmin's own page for today's terms before you buy.

Is Oura subscription-free?

No — Oura sells a membership, listed on its own Member Care page at $5.99/month and $69.99/year in the US. What that page also says is that without an active membership you can still see the three daily Oura scores (Readiness, Activity and Sleep), plus ring battery, basic profile information, app settings and the Explore content. So the daily number survives; the explanation around it does not.

Do I need to buy anything if I already own a watch?

Probably not. If your wearable writes heart-rate variability, resting heart rate and sleep to Apple Health, a free app can read them and give you a daily recovery score without any new hardware — that is what Summit does for cyclists, permanently free, with no trial and no card. If you are not a cyclist, check your watch maker's own app first: it may already show a similar daily figure at no extra cost.

Is Summit free, or is it a trial?

Free, permanently, for what it says: the daily recovery score, its 30-day trend, the sleep score with its full breakdown and the Home and Lock Screen widgets. That is a free tier, not a countdown. Premium is separate — training load, power analysis, the prescribed session, export and race-day fuelling — priced at 7.99 euros a month or 63.99 euros a year for Pro, and 14.99 euros a month or 119.99 euros a year for Elite, the Spanish App Store listing checked 7 August 2026. Its 14-day trial is an introductory offer for new subscribers. Apple sets the price per storefront, so yours may differ.
